Who’s working on you?

A Singapore-registered buyer, a chip-maker’s CEO and a spy’s playbook. The Nvidia story is about a giant, but the method works best on owners who decide alone. Here is how it works and what a small firm can check.

the short version

  • The weak point is rarely the computer system. It is one trusted person who can say yes on their own, and that is usually the owner.
  • The method is patient: study the person, arrange a “chance” meeting, flatter their work, become the one who understands them, then quietly push out anyone who doubts.
  • In the Nvidia case, the questions came after the orders. A big new buyer with thin substance behind it is the oldest warning sign there is.
  • Your gatekeepers (PA, driver, admin, IT person) hear everything. Spies go for them because they feel unseen and cost little.
  • Two cheap habits block most of this: nobody approves a big new customer or payment change alone, and when a trusted sceptic objects, you ask why before you act.

the case: Nvidia and Megaspeed

I use this case because it is current, it involves a company registered in Singapore, and the lessons scale down. The allegations of spying are not proven. I have marked what is on the record, what is reported from unnamed sources, and what is disputed.

On the recordReported, unnamed sourcesDisputed or denied
  1. 2025reported
    The New York Times reports Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company and Nvidia customer, is suspected of helping Chinese firms get around US export limits on AI chips. US officials visiting its Malaysia data centre reportedly found Nvidia equipment still in boxes.
  2. 2025 to nowdisputed
    Nvidia says the diversion allegations against Megaspeed were investigated and debunked, that Megaspeed is one of many regional cloud customers, and that meetings with its executives were routine.
  3. Spring 2026reported
    According to the Times, a classified US memo warns officials about Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), a Megaspeed executive, based on about a year of surveillance.
  4. 29 Oct 2026reported
    The Times reports US officials believe Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset passing commercial information to Beijing, and that Jensen Huang was not believed to know. A Times reporter later said the company she ran bought nearly US$5 billion of Nvidia chip technology.
  5. 8 Nov 2026on the record
    Jensen Huang tells staff by email that he had a relationship and will cooperate fully. Nvidia’s board opens an independent inquiry and says it has no reason to believe trade secrets or national security were compromised.
  6. 9 Nov 2026denied
    Nvidia shares fall about 8% at the US open. China’s foreign ministry rejects the spying claim. Megaspeed had not replied to press questions at the time of the reports.

What an SME owner can take from it

  • The boss’s yes skips the checksA former CIA officer on the podcast below argues nobody vetted the buyer because the order came from the top. In a 20-person firm, that is every order.
  • Check what stands behind a buyerReporting questioned what Megaspeed actually ran. Real customers have offices, staff and a history you can see.
  • Pillow talk travels further than chipsThe bigger risk named on the podcast is not the product. It is what a trusted person hears about plans, prices and other clients.
  • Security spend misses peopleA large company can guard its chief executive and still miss who is close to him. Small firms rarely guard either.

the playbook, in eight steps

This is the sequence Aliia Roza, who says she was trained as a Russian “honey trap” operative, walked through on the podcast. It doesn’t need romance: the same steps work for a “mentor”, a “partner” or a very keen new customer.

  1. ReconIn an SMEYour LinkedIn, ACRA record, staff posts and trade-fair photos tell a stranger who decides, what you care about and who you trust.FlagA new contact already knows your story, your hobbies and your schedule.
  2. Engineer the meetingIn an SMEA warm introduction at an event, a friend of a friend, a buyer who “happens” to sit beside you. Roza’s rule: never approach the target first.FlagYou can’t say who arranged the first meeting, or it was too lucky.
  3. Love bombIn an SMEPraise for what you built, not how you look. Then a small favour (“could you look at my plan?”), which makes you invested and feel valued.FlagAdmiration that is specific, constant and arrives very early.
  4. Mirror and anchorIn an SMEThey copy your posture, words and pace, and tie good feelings to small cues: a phrase, a scent, a place.FlagToo many “me too”s. Change your gesture and see if they follow.
  5. Information controlIn an SMEThey turn up at your events, join your WhatsApp groups and get to know your key staff and clients.FlagThey are suddenly everywhere your business is.
  6. Emotional dependencyIn an SMEYou feel sharper, younger, understood. You take their calls first. It feels like a great partnership.Flag“Only they really get what I’m building.”
  7. IsolationIn an SMENever a direct attack. Hints: “I heard your accountant said…” You push the sceptic away and think it was your idea.FlagYour partner, spouse or longest-serving staff have gone quiet about them.
  8. GaslightingIn an SMEYour own checks start to feel like paranoia. Facts get re-told until you doubt what you remember.Flag“You’re overthinking it” whenever you ask a normal question.

where a small firm is exposed

You don’t need to make chips to be worth working on. Tender prices, customer lists, supplier terms, access to a bigger client or a government contract, and goods that are export-controlled are all worth the effort.

  • The ownerDecides alone, signs fast, and is the person staff are least likely to question.
  • The gatekeepersPA, driver, admin and the IT person see diaries, invoices and passwords, and are often the least thanked.
  • The keen new buyerA big first order, an urgent timeline, and not much you can check behind the name.
  • The quiet scepticThe partner, spouse, accountant or long-serving manager whose doubts slowly stop being heard.

red flags

  • They already know a lot about you at the first meeting.
  • Everything you like, they like too.
  • Praise for your work that is constant and specific, early on.
  • They copy your posture and pace, and follow when you change it.
  • Small favours that grow into a mentor or partner role.
  • They appear at your events, groups and client meetings.
  • Your trusted people have gone quiet about them.
  • You feel foolish for asking basic questions.
  • A big order or deal that skips your usual checks because you said so.
  • Questions about other clients, prices or plans that have nothing to do with the deal.

five checks to start this month

  1. 1
    Look behind every big new buyer. Check the ACRA record, a real office, real staff and a history older than the deal. My Pre-visit Fact Sheet does the first part from a UEN. Visit if the order matters.
  2. 2
    No one approves alone, including you. A big new customer, an unusual order or a change of bank details needs a second person to say yes. In the Nvidia story the checks reportedly fell away because the top said go.
  3. 3
    Treat the sceptic as an alarm, not a nuisance. When your partner, accountant or a long-serving staff member doesn’t like a new contact, ask what they noticed before you decide.
  4. 4
    Look after your gatekeepers. Thank them, tell them what is sensitive, and make it easy to say “someone was asking about…” without feeling disloyal.
  5. 5
    Know if what you supply is controlled. Electronics, chips and some industrial goods can need a permit to move on. Check with Singapore Customs on strategic goods, and see Who Wants What You Supply? if you serve defence, government or critical industries.
