Twenty years of trying tools

What I signed up for since 2006, what lasted, what I let go, and how I pick tools now.

the short version

  • I’ve used Gmail since early 2006, when you still needed an invite to join. Most tools since then left a receipt or a sign-up email, so this page comes from my inbox, not from memory.
  • I try tools early and I let them go when they stop earning their place. Many lasted a year or two. The few that lasted much longer each did one job well.
  • I was paying for an AI writing tool in 2020 and an AI image tool in September 2022, two months before ChatGPT came out. AI is the newest wave I’ve tried, not the first.

what I tried, and what it taught me

  • 2006
    files

    My inbox as a hard drive

    GmailFS, then Google Docs & Spreadsheets, later Dropbox

    tried
    Before Dropbox existed, a small program let me save files into my Gmail account and open them on any computer. The same year I started sharing plans as Google Docs instead of email attachments.
    kept
    The idea, not the hack. Dropbox replaced it in 2010, and I still write in shared documents.
    learned
    I wanted my work on any machine. The first tool that does that is rarely the one that lasts.
  • 2008 to 2011
    phones

    Two phones in one pocket

    BlackBerry for work email, an early iPhone for everything else

    tried
    Bought an early iPhone in late 2008 and carried it next to the work BlackBerry. From 2009 I was the trainer who taught a call centre each new iPhone before the launch queues formed.
    learned
    Using a device on my own time first is how I learned to teach it to people who had to support it.
  • 2009 to now
    show, don’t tell

    Recording the screen to explain things

    Jing and a Camtasia trial in 2009, Snagit from 2012, video editing apps later

    tried
    Short screen recordings in place of long written instructions. Snagit came in 2012 and I’ve upgraded it twice since.
    kept
    The longest-running paid tool on this page, and the habit behind the explainer videos I make today.
    learned
    Two minutes of showing saves a page of telling.
  • 2009 to 2010
    switching

    Moving to a Mac without leaving Windows

    Mac mini, then a MacBook Pro, with Parallels Desktop to run Windows

    tried
    Moved my own computing to a Mac, and kept the Windows programs I still needed running alongside.
    learned
    Run the old and the new side by side until the new one covers the whole job. Nobody has to switch overnight.
  • 2011 to 2019
    learning

    Paying for my own lessons

    Kelby Training (monthly, for photography), Udemy, Lynda.com, Coursera, edX

    tried
    Online courses on my own account, years before any employer asked me to.
    learned
    A course sticks when I use it on a real job the same week.
  • 2007 to now
    notes

    Where my thinking lives

    MindManager trial, MindMeister, Evernote Premium, Notion, now Obsidian

    tried
    Mind maps from 2007. MindMeister stayed eight years. Evernote Premium came and went twice, Notion lasted a year, and in 2026 I moved my notes to plain files on my own computer.
    learned
    Notes outlive the app they were written in. Keep them in a form you can take with you.
  • 2020 to 2023
    early AI

    AI that did one small job

    Wordtune (2020), ClipDrop (2020), Midjourney (2022), Otter.ai, QuillBot, ChatGPT Plus (2023)

    tried
    Rewording a sentence, cutting out a background, transcribing a meeting, making an image from a description. Each did one narrow thing.
    learned
    Trust AI with one small, checkable job first. The big promises can wait until the small ones hold up.
  • 2025 to now
    private data

    AI that stays on my own machine

    AI models that run offline on a laptop

    tried
    Running AI models on my own laptop, with nothing sent out, for documents that shouldn’t leave it. The full story is in choosing the laptop.
    learned
    Not every job needs the biggest model. Some just need one that stays at home.

kept, and let go

kept for years

  • Gmail 2006–now
  • Shared documents 2006–now
  • Snagit, screen capture 2012–now
  • MindMeister, mind maps 2013–2021
  • Notability, handwritten notes 2013–now

let go

  • Evernote Premium 2014–2017
  • Medium membership 2018–2019
  • Asana Premium 2019–2021
  • Padlet Pro 2020–2021
  • Notion 2022–2023
  • ChatGPT Plus 2023–2026

Letting a tool go isn’t a failure. It usually means another tool now does that job, or the job changed.

how I’d pick a tool for a team

  • start with one job

    Name the task that eats the most time. Pick a tool for that, not a tool for everything.

  • try it on real work

    A month on the actual job tells you more than a demo.

  • keep your files portable

    If you leave the tool, your work should come with you.

  • mind the data

    Customer and staff records go only to tools you’d be comfortable telling those people about.

  • cancel without guilt

    Check before each renewal. If nobody would miss it, let it go.
