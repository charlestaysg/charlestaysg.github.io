Show what you can do

Singapore employers are being asked to look at what people can do, not only the papers they hold. Here is how to make skills easy to see and check, whether you hire, switch jobs or are just starting.

the short version

  • In October 2026 the heads of the Singapore Business Federation, SNEF, NTUC and SUSS, with the chief executive of the Skills and Workforce Development Agency (SWDA), put out a paper on Skills-First: looking at what a person can do when you hire, design jobs, move people around, train them and promote them.
  • Qualifications still count. They stop being the only thing that counts.
  • It is a discussion paper with pilot ideas, not a rule or a grant. Nobody has to do it yet.
  • Most of the change has to come from employers. But a skill nobody can see doesn’t get used, so it pays to make yours easy to check.

Jump to your part: I run a small business · I’m mid-career · I’ve just graduated

three things per skill

The paper asks people to describe their experience as skills, keep a record of evidence, and get recommendations from people who saw the work. That comes down to three things for every skill you want noticed.

1 · claim

Say the skill in the employer’s words

Use the names in your sector’s Skills Framework, the national list other employers and the Careers & Skills Passport use. “Data analysis and visualisation”, not “Excel guru”.

2 · proof

Show something that happened

A work sample, a before-and-after number, an assessment, a test result. Something a stranger can look at.

3 · witness

Name someone who saw it

A supervisor, client or lecturer who can say “I saw them do this task”, and names the task.

pick your door

Four of the five decisions happen inside your company: designing jobs, moving people, training them, promoting them. LinkedIn doesn’t help with any of those. What helps is a written record of what each person has been seen doing.

  • 1 · write the job as tasks

    Rewrite one job ad

    List the five tasks the person will do in the first three months, with the standard you expect. Keep the qualification line if you want; just stop letting it decide on its own.

    wasHardworking, diploma preferred, good communication.
    nowPick and pack 150 orders a day with fewer than two wrong items a week. Call back customers about late orders the same day.

  • 2 · test the task

    A 30-minute work sample at interview

    Give every candidate a small, made-up version of the real task. Score everyone on the same three or four points, written down before the first interview, so the result doesn’t depend on who happens to be interviewing.

  • 3 · sign off what staff can do

    One sheet per person

    Task, date seen, who saw it, standard met. Fill it in when a supervisor watches someone do the task properly. Look at it before you move anyone, send them on a course or promote them. The sheet is below; print it.

  • 4 · borrow the official words

    Use the Skills Framework for your sector

    Write tasks in your own words first. Then add the matching skill name from the Skills Framework ↗, so a record means the same thing to the next employer or a training provider. My Skills Training Landscape page shows the courses listed against the roles still open in each sector.

  • why bother

    Someone you have seen do the job is a safer bet

    Hiring a stranger with the right certificate costs money and carries risk. One estimate in the series puts an SME’s cost of screening a hire at about 14% of a year’s salary. A person you have already watched doing the task is cheaper to move or promote, and less likely to surprise you.

You have years of skill, but your CV probably describes it as job titles, and your best work belongs to your employer. The job is translation: titles into tasks, tasks into words employers search for.

  • 1 · titles into tasks

    Three to five tasks per job, each with a result

    A job title says where you sat. A task with a result says what you can do.

    wasSenior Executive, Operations, 2016–2025.
    nowCut the weekly stock count from six hours to two by redoing the bin layout and labels.

  • 2 · borrow the official words

    Match your tasks to the Skills Framework

    Find your sector in the Skills Framework ↗ and put its skill name next to each task. Recruiters’ systems and the Careers & Skills Passport use those names. Switching sectors? Check which skills appear in both.

  • 3 · prove it quietly

    Write up confidential work without names

    Half a page per piece of work: the problem, what you did, what changed. Leave out client names, prices and anything that identifies the company. That is enough for an interviewer to ask good questions.

  • 4 · line up witnesses

    Ask for recommendations before you leave

    Ask a supervisor or client to name the task they saw you do, while they still remember it. The paper notes that automated CV screening drops good candidates. A direct recommendation goes around it.

  • 5 · once a year

    A skills stock-take

    List what you added this year and what has gone stale. One paper in the series describes a reliable worker passed over because they hadn’t added new skills. It also cites a Singapore study where only a quarter of workers made planned job moves that paid off.

You don’t have years of work to point to, so your proof comes from what you did while studying. Most graduates have more of it than they think. It just isn’t written down.

  • 1 · every project is a sample

    Half a page for each one

    Internship, work-study, final-year project, a CCA you ran. Write the brief, what you did yourself, the result, and what you would do differently next time.

    wasCompleted a 6-month internship at a logistics company.
    nowBuilt the weekly late-delivery report in Excel; the team used it to cut late orders from 9% to 5% over my last two months.

  • 2 · ask your school

    Does it issue a skills record?

    Some schools now issue a skills-based or co-curricular transcript alongside grades. SMU has had one since 2025. If yours does, attach it. If it doesn’t, your half-page write-ups do the same job.

  • 3 · one witness per project

    Someone who saw you do it

    An internship supervisor or lecturer who will let you name them. Ask them now, while they remember the work.

  • 4 · make one thing in public

    Small and finished beats big and unfinished

    GitHub for code or data, a portfolio page for design, a two-minute video for hands-on work. One finished piece in your field gives an interviewer something real to ask about.

  • 5 · check the words

    Use the words the job ads use

    Read ten job ads for the role you want and look up the role in the Skills Framework ↗. Use the same skill names on your CV and LinkedIn.

what this won’t fix

Making skills visible only helps if employers look. The first paper in the series cites a US finding: for every 100 jobs that dropped a degree requirement, fewer than four more people without degrees were hired. Changing the job ad wasn’t enough on its own.

So the SME door matters most. A worker can build a perfect record, but a company that writes jobs as tasks and signs off what staff can do is the part that changes who gets hired and promoted.
